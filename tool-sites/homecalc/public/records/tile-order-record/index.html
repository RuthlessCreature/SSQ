Worked example | Updated November 26, 2026

A tile order is a layout decision disguised as square footage.

This fictional bathroom-floor scenario shows why a net floor area and a carton count are only the beginning. It keeps field area, planned cuts, carton coverage, trim, and repair stock in separate decisions.

Scenario: rectangular bathroom floorFinish scope: field tile onlyOutput: whole cartons

1. Record the tileable field, then state the omissions

The sample room measures 7 ft 6 in by 8 ft 0 in. A 30 in by 21 in vanity footprint is excluded because it will remain fixed in place. The doorway is not subtracted: the tile still needs cuts and a finished edge in that zone. This record estimates field tile only; it does not attempt to calculate underlayment, waterproofing, mortar, grout, movement joints, edge trim, or transition profiles.

In this example the room's length includes the tileable part of the threshold. If your measurement stops before that line, record the threshold as a separate positive rectangle. Keep deductions as negative rows so someone can verify which footprints you excluded.

2. Choose an allowance for the actual layout

The room has four perimeter cut lines, a doorway interruption, and a vanity return. For a straight layout the example uses 12% as a budgeting assumption so the two carton sizes can be compared on equal terms. The adjusted quantity is 55.625 x 1.12 = 62.30 sq ft. Revisit the allowance after sketching the layout for each tile format.

That 12% is an explicit planning hypothesis, not a universal tile rule. A centered layout that leaves narrow perimeter strips, a diagonal pattern, a large pattern variation, or a product with limited replacement availability can justify a different decision. A simple square room with a forgiving tile size may justify less. Draw the dry layout or obtain installer input before changing the number.

3. Convert adjusted area to a carton decision

The Daltile ec1 sales sheet lists 15.49 sq ft per carton for 24 x 24 field tile and 11.62 sq ft for 12 x 24 field tile. These are published packaging figures. The room, allowance and prices below are constructed for the example. Check the supplier's current carton label, availability and product suitability separately.

Four large cartons cover 61.96 sq ft, leaving a 0.34 sq ft shortage. Rounding the nominal tile name to four square feet and multiplying by four tiles per box would instead imply 16 sq ft per carton. That shortcut would make four boxes look sufficient. Use the label's carton coverage for the order and the actual tile size for layout.

When the choice changes

With the room unchanged, four large cartons support at most about 11.39% allowance. At 11% the target is 61.74375 sq ft and they cover it. At 12% they do not. Keep the allowance tied to a dry layout. Lowering it solely to avoid another carton hides the shortfall.

At the illustrative quotes the larger format costs $10 less, but it buys 7.73 more square feet. At the same 12% allowance, the six-carton option becomes cheaper below roughly $48.33 per carton. A shipping difference of more than $10 can reverse the example's ranking. If the formats need different allowances, run each separately and compare the resulting totals.

If the vanity will be replaced and the new exposed footprint is uncertain, test the entire 60 sq ft floor. With the same allowance the target is 67.2 sq ft. Both options still require five and six cartons respectively. That makes the vanity decision useful for setting material and layout even though it does not change the field-tile order here.

4. What this calculation cannot decide

Square footage does not determine the correct substrate, waterproofing assembly, mortar, grout, layout line, or movement treatment. Those choices depend on the location, product instructions, existing conditions, and sometimes local requirements. The record intentionally leaves them outside the field-tile calculation rather than presenting a quantity estimate as an installation plan.

  • Confirm each non-field item separately: trim pieces, transition, edge treatment, setting materials, grout, and any required system components.
  • Check actual tile dimensions and carton coverage at the supplier before relying on a saved estimate.
  • Revisit the allowance when the layout is centered, rotated, includes a border, or creates thin cuts.
  • Record the product identifier and production information needed for future matching before the job starts.
  • Seek project-specific advice for wet areas, substrate issues, or conditions beyond an early material order.
