Tile guide

Bathroom tile buying checklist.

Bathrooms often have more cuts than open rooms. Plan tile, trim, grout, waterproofing, and spare pieces together.

Measure the bathroom in separate zones

Bathroom tile estimates are easy to undercount because the room is small but detailed. Floors, shower walls, tub surrounds, niches, backsplashes, curbs, and thresholds may use different tile sizes or trim pieces. Measure each zone separately before combining the order.

Do not rely only on the floor area. A bathroom with a 40 sq ft floor can still have hundreds of square feet of wall tile if the shower area is tiled to the ceiling.

Before you order

  • Confirm whether floor tile and wall tile are the same product.
  • Check slip rating and suitability for wet areas.
  • Confirm box coverage, pieces per box, and dye lot availability.
  • Count niche, curb, threshold, and transition pieces.
  • Keep at least a few full spare tiles for future repairs.

Example workflow

Start with the floor area, then add each tiled wall face. Apply the waste rate to each tile type separately. Convert square footage to pieces, then convert pieces to full boxes. Finally, add trim by linear length and waterproofing materials by product coverage.

This separate-zone method takes a few more minutes, but it prevents a common mistake: ordering enough field tile while forgetting edge trim, niche pieces, or extra material for cuts.

Bathroom note: water exposure makes product compatibility important. Use the calculator for quantity planning, then confirm installation materials with the tile supplier or installer.

Planning method

Building a bathroom tile shopping list around the whole assembly

Bathroom tile is not a tile-only purchase. Floors, shower walls, niches, trim, waterproofing, substrate preparation, grout, sealants, and transitions interact. A complete checklist starts with the surfaces and ends with a coordinated installation system.

A repeatable field workflow

  1. List each tiled surface separately: floor, shower walls, tub surround, niche, bench, curb, backsplash, and feature wall. Each may use different tile, trim, waterproofing detail, and waste allowance.
  2. Measure the surfaces and choose the tile layout before ordering. Small bathrooms contain many fixtures and edges, so a simple square-foot number can understate cuts and trim.
  3. Select a compatible waterproofing and setting system for wet areas, then confirm substrate requirements. Tile and grout are not waterproofing by themselves.
  4. Add trim, edge profiles, grout, spacers, clips, thinset, sealant, transitions, drain details, tools, protection, and cleanup to the material plan.
  5. Order matching repair pieces and verify shade, caliber, and finish before opening boxes. Returns and replacements are harder after wet-area work begins.

Worked planning example

A bathroom floor may be only 40 square feet, but it can require more planning than a larger open room because of the toilet flange, vanity, doorway, threshold, and perimeter cuts. A shower adds wall tile, niches, corners, trim, waterproofing, and drain or curb details. Treat each zone as its own line item, then combine the purchase list after layout and product selection are clear.

Common planning errors

  • Ordering tile without trim and edge details.
  • Treating grout as waterproofing.
  • Using one waste rate for all bathroom surfaces.
  • Ignoring shade and caliber across cartons.
  • Leaving sealant, transitions, and preparation out of the budget.
Scope boundary: This is a shopping and planning checklist. Wet-area construction, waterproofing, drainage, electrical work, permits, and code requirements require appropriate professional or manufacturer guidance.

Use the estimate well

Keep the Building a bathroom tile shopping list around the whole assembly guide connected to the real job

A useful guide is a record of a specific room, material, layout, and date. Write down the measurements, product package size, coverage or yield, and any allowance you selected. Without that record, a number copied into a shopping cart becomes hard to check when a wall changes, a carton has different coverage, or the selected product is out of stock.

The recommendation should be revised whenever the assumptions change. That is not a failure of the original calculation; it is how an early planning estimate stays honest. Keep the first version, note what changed, and compare the new purchase quantity with the exact package sizes and return policy before ordering.

Recheck the estimate when any of these change

  • The measured surface gains or loses an opening, alcove, border, closet, or separate material zone.
  • The layout changes direction, pattern, joint spacing, strip height, board length, or finished termination point.
  • The product package, usable coverage, yield, repeat, actual dimension, or installation instructions differ from the first assumption.
  • The site inspection reveals repairs, uneven surfaces, moisture, access limits, or a condition that adds preparation or waste.
  • The estimate sits close to the next carton, roll, panel, bucket, or stock-length boundary.

Three questions before you place an order

What number should I bring to the store?

Bring the raw measured quantity, the allowance and why you chose it, the adjusted quantity, and the final number of purchasable units. A store associate or installer can help check a transparent estimate; they cannot reliably check a single unexplained total.

Should I always buy extra?

Buy a reasoned reserve for cuts, package rounding, defects, and repairs, not an arbitrary pile of material. The value of same-batch spare material is higher when a product is special order, patterned, dyed, discontinued, or difficult to match.

When should I stop and ask for help?

Pause when the work involves structural loading, waterproofing, drainage, electrical systems, plumbing, fire ratings, permits, hazardous materials, or an active building failure. Material quantity is not a substitute for a safe scope of work.
